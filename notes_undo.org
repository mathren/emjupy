#+TITLE: Undo in emjupy
#+SUBTITLE: What it does today, why, and what it would take to do better

* The short version

Undo works within a cell until something redraws the notebook.  A redraw
discards the undo history, so edits made before it cannot be undone --
though they are not lost, they are still in the buffer and in the saved
notebook.

A redraw happens when a cell is run, when output arrives from any cell,
and when cells are added, deleted, moved, split or merged.

This is a real gap against both =ein= and JupyterLab, and it is a
deliberate trade rather than an oversight.  The rest of this file says
what the trade is.

* Measured behaviour

Typing =+ 99= into the second cell of a two-cell notebook, then asking
what undo can do:

| Situation                             | Undo entries | =C-/= does                      |
|---------------------------------------+--------------+-------------------------------|
| No redraw in between                  |            2 | undoes the typing             |
| After output arrives for ANOTHER cell |            0 | "No further undo information" |

In the second row the typed text is still there -- output arriving syncs
the buffer into the cells before redrawing, so nothing is lost -- but the
history describing how it got there is gone.

That second row is the one that matters in practice: you are editing
cell 5 while cell 1 is still running, its plot lands, and your last few
edits are no longer undoable.

* Why it is like this

Rendering erases the whole buffer and rebuilds it from the cell structs.
Two things follow, and the second is the awkward one.

** Recording the rebuild is worse than not recording it

The first version let the rebuild go into the undo history.  A single
redraw pushed about twenty entries, and undoing one of them tore the
notebook apart -- deleting every cell after the one being edited, because
an undo entry described a deletion of a region that, by then, meant
something else entirely.  Rendering is therefore done with
=buffer-undo-list= bound to =t=, which keeps it out of the history.

** But then the history that remains is describing a buffer that no longer exists

Emacs undo entries hold plain buffer positions -- =(BEG . END)= pairs and
bare integers -- not markers.  Nothing adjusts them when text moves
around while undo recording is off.  After a rebuild, an entry recorded
before it may point anywhere.  Replaying it does not fail loudly; it
quietly changes the wrong text.

So the choice is between losing the history and risking silent
corruption.  emjupy currently clears =buffer-undo-list= when a rebuild
actually changed the text, and leaves it alone when the text came out
identical.  Losing undo is bad.  Corrupting a notebook is worse.

* How the others avoid it

** JupyterLab

Each cell is a separate CodeMirror editor with its own history.  There is
no shared buffer for a redraw to invalidate, so the question does not
arise.  Nothing in Emacs gives this for free: a notebook here is one
buffer.

** ein

=ein= keeps the single-buffer design and does the bookkeeping, which is
worth reading before assuming it is easy.  From =ein-worksheet.el=:

- =ein:%which-cell%= is a parallel list, one entry per =buffer-undo-list=
  entry, recording which cell each change belonged to.  It is maintained
  in an =after-change-functions= hook whose own docstring opens "This is a
  costly after-change-functions hook."
- =ein:worksheet--jigger-undo-list= shifts the positions inside undo
  entries by a signed distance when a cell's length changes.  Doing that
  correctly means handling every shape an undo entry can take: integers,
  =(BEG . END)=, =(TEXT . POS)=, markers, =(nil PROP VAL BEG . END)= and
  =(apply ...)=.  The function is headed:

  #+begin_quote
  Nobody said it was easy.
  No one ever said it would be this hard.
  #+end_quote

- =buffer-undo-list= is pruned at 2000 entries, because Emacs garbage
  collects it by *bytes* and elisp cannot see that number.
- =ein:worksheet-enable-undo= covers "cell inputs only (as opposed to
  whole-cell operations such as killing, moving, executing cells)".

That last point is worth noting: even ein, having done all this work,
scopes undo to edits *within* a cell and not to structural operations.
Whatever emjupy does should aim for the same scope rather than something
more ambitious.

* What it would take here

In increasing order of risk.

** 1. Redraw only what changed

The root problem is that any change redraws everything.  Output arriving
for one cell rebuilds the entire notebook, including cells nowhere near
it.

There is already a working precedent in the codebase: the execution
spinner updates =[In: |]= purely through =overlay-put= on a
=before-string=, so it changes no buffer text at all -- invisible to undo,
and it leaves the history intact.  Output is real text and cannot be
done that way, but it can be patched in place within its own cell's
region instead of triggering a full rebuild.

This is worth doing on its own merits, undo aside.

** 2. Truncate the history by position, not wholesale

Once a redraw touches only one region, entries positioned entirely
*before* that region are still valid and can be kept.  That alone covers
the common case: editing cell 5 while cell 1 produces output, since
cell 1 is earlier in the buffer.

Worst case it is over-cautious and drops more than strictly necessary --
the same failure as today, but narrower.

** 3. Shift the entries that follow

Entries positioned *after* the edit could be adjusted by the size delta
rather than discarded, which is what ein's =jigger-undo-list= does.  This
is the piece that would make "typing in cell 2 while cell 5 produces
output" survive as well.

It is also where an off-by-one stops being a cosmetic bug: a wrong
position does not raise an error, it silently rewrites the wrong text.
That is worse than the current behaviour, so it needs deciding
deliberately and testing hard -- not bundling with step 2.

** An alternative: leave =buffer-undo-list= out of it

Track each cell's edits separately, in the cell struct, and give
=emjupy-undo= its own command that only ever replays within the cell at
point.  This is what JupyterLab does in effect, and it is immune to the
whole class of position bugs, since nothing is keyed by buffer position.

It means reimplementing undo and redo, including the parts people expect
without thinking about them -- redo after undo, boundaries that match
what a user considers "one change", interaction with =undo-tree= if they
use it.  A larger project than steps 1 to 3, and the only route to a
guarantee rather than a good approximation.

* Where this stands

Nothing here is implemented.  Step 1 is the one to do first: it is
bounded, it helps performance regardless, and steps 2 and 3 are not
possible without it.  Whether step 3 is worth its risk should be decided
after step 2 is in use, when it is clear how often the remaining gap is
actually hit.
